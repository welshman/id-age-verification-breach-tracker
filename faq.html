FAQ, methodology & ethics

What this site is

What does this site do?

It tracks publicly reported data breaches involving identity documents (passports, national ID cards, driver's licences) and the companies that process them for identity verification, Know Your Customer (KYC) compliance, or age verification. It is inspired by Have I Been Pwned but is entirely independent, not affiliated with HIBP, and focused specifically on identity/age-verification data rather than emails and passwords.

What this site is not
  • It is not a way to check whether your specific passport or ID number has leaked — we never collect or process actual document numbers.
  • It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or built using non-public data from any government, verification vendor, or Have I Been Pwned.
  • It is not a complete record of every breach that has ever happened — many incidents are never publicly disclosed.
  • It is not intended to shame any individual company; the goal is public awareness, not fear-mongering.

How data is collected and updated

Where does the breach data come from?

Every breach listed is sourced from public reporting: investigative journalism, established security researchers, regulatory findings (FTC, ICO, CNBV, AGCO), and direct statements from the affected companies. Each breach entry links to its original sources so you can verify the claim yourself.

How is the site updated without manual editing?

A scheduled GitHub Actions workflow (.github/workflows/update-data.yml) runs periodically and:

  • Scans configured security-news RSS feeds and (optionally) the Have I Been Pwned API for new items matching identity/age-verification keywords defined in data/update-config.json.
  • Writes any new candidate matches to data/pending-review.json rather than publishing them directly, since automated keyword matching can produce false positives on a topic this sensitive.
  • Opens or updates a GitHub Issue summarizing new candidates so a maintainer can confirm details and merge them into breaches.json / companies.json via a normal pull request.

This means new candidates are discovered automatically, while final publication of a claimed breach still passes through lightweight human confirmation — a deliberate design choice to avoid publishing unverified rumors about real companies and real victims.

Why not fully auto-publish every match with zero human involvement?

Falsely claiming a company suffered a data breach can cause real reputational and legal harm, and keyword-matched news feeds regularly produce false positives (e.g. articles that merely mention a company's name near the word "breach"). We treat accuracy as more important than being 100% "hands-off," so the pipeline automates discovery and drafting, while a lightweight review step protects against publishing incorrect claims.

Privacy & ethics

Do you store any of my personal data?

No. This site has no backend server and no database. It only reads pre-generated public data files and displays them; it does not collect, store, or process any personal information from visitors, and we never ask for actual ID or passport numbers.

Are you trying to scare people away from age verification?

No. The goal is informed awareness, not alarmism. Age and identity verification serve legitimate purposes (child safety, fraud prevention, regulatory compliance). This site simply documents that any system storing or transmitting identity documents carries breach risk, so users and policymakers can make informed choices about which services to trust and what safeguards to demand.

Limitations

What are the known limitations of this tracker?
  • Incomplete coverage: Many breaches, especially outside major English-language media markets, are never publicly reported or are disclosed only to regulators.
  • Disputed attribution: Some entries (e.g. certain credit-bureau exposures) involve companies that dispute the leaked data originated from their own systems; we note this explicitly where it applies.
  • Not all entries are data breaches: A small number of entries (such as regulatory fines for age-verification control failures) document compliance failures rather than confirmed data leaks; these are clearly labelled in their details.
  • Reporting lag: Some breaches are discovered privately months before public disclosure, so our timeline reflects disclosure dates, not necessarily when an incident began.
  • Company inclusion ≠ wrongdoing: Most companies listed on the companies page have no known breach; they're included to provide a comprehensive picture of the identity-verification industry.
  • Severity ratings are editorial judgments based on data sensitivity and scale, not an official industry standard.

Protecting yourself

General steps if your ID document may have been exposed
  • Watch for notification emails from services you've used, and verify their authenticity before clicking links.
  • Monitor bank and credit accounts for unfamiliar activity, and consider a credit freeze or fraud alert where available in your country.
  • If your passport, driver's licence, or national ID was exposed, contact the issuing government authority to ask about reissuance, flagging, or monitoring options.
  • Be cautious about which services you upload ID documents to in the future; check whether a company has a history of breaches on this site first.
  • Be alert to identity-theft attempts using your exposed data, such as new accounts, loan applications, or SIM-swap attempts.
